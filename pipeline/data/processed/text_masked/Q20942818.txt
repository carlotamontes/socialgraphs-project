AMANDA   Halzen first learned about attempts by Russian scientists to detect neutrinos in Antarctica, using radio antennas at their Antarctic research station to search for electric sparks resulting from cosmic neutrinos colliding with the ice.
After determining these interactions would be too weak to register, in 1987 PRON started working on the AMANDA project, which proposed burying an array of light sensors deep in the Antarctic ice which is clear, dark, stable, sterile and free of background light.
That design was based on an idea by Soviet physicist NAME, originally proposed in 1960 but never implemented, to look for high-energy neutrinos by putting NAME detectors into Lake Baikal.
This pilot AMANDA experiment was able to detect high-energy cosmic muons and neutrinos, but its measurements were affected by interference from cosmic rays, as well as by air bubbles in the ice.
These factors convinced NAME that a much larger and deeper array would be needed, and in 2005 the AMANDA project became part of its successor project, the IceCube Neutrino Observatory.
IceCube Neutrino Observatory   In 2005, PRON team began construction of the IceCube project, designed to be 100 times larger than AMANDA, with a total volume of  and buried up to  deep.
After six years of construction, IceCube became fully operational in 2011.
The most important result from IceCube was the clear breakthrough observation of high-energy neutrinos (about 100 times more energetic than the particles accelerated today at the world's most powerful machine, the LHC at CERN) in 2013, from as-yet-unidentified sources outside the galaxy.
This discovery has stimulated the planning and development of even larger neutrino telescopes, both at the South Pole and deep underwater.

Nobel Prize in Physics  In 2026, NAME was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin."
In announcing the award, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences credited NAME's scientific leadership in developing IceCube as an instrument for neutrino astronomy.
It explained that high-energy neutrinos can point us to distant astrophysical particle accelerators, because neutrinos travel over cosmic distances without changing direction or losing energy.
