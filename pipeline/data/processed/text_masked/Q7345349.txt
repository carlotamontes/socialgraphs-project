Homochirality  Organic molecules often come in two mirror-image forms, often referred to as "right-handed" and "left-handed".
This handedness is called chirality.
For example, the amino acid alanine comes in a right-handed (D-alanine) and a left-handed (L-alanine) form.
Living cells are very selective, choosing amino acids only in the left-handed form and sugars in the right-handed form.
However, most abiotic processes produce an equal amount of each.
Somehow life must have developed this preference (homochirality); but while scientists have proposed several theories, they have no consensus on the mechanism.
NAME investigated the possibility that organic molecules might acquire a chiral asymmetry when grown on the faces of mineral crystals.
Some, like quartz, come in mirror-image forms; others, like calcite, are symmetric about their centers but their faces come in pairs with opposite chirality.
With NAME, an expert at organic chemical analysis, and NAME, a geochemist, Hazen cleaned large calcite crystals and dipped them into aspartic acid.
They found that each face of the crystal had a small preference for either left- or right-handed forms of aspartate.
They proposed that a similar mechanism might work on other amino acids and sugars.
This work attracted a lot of interest from the pharmaceutical industry, which needs to produce some of their drugs with a pure chirality.
Mineral evolution  At a Christmas party in 2006, the biophysicist NAME asked NAME whether there were clay minerals during the Archean Eon.

The result was a paper in American Mineralogist that provided a new historical context to mineralogy that they called mineral evolution.
Based on a review of the literature, NAME and PRON co-authors estimated that the number of minerals in the Solar System has grown from about a dozen at the time of its formation to over 4300 at present. (
As of 2026, the latter number has grown to 6239.).
They predicted that there was a systematic increase in the number of mineral species over time, and identified three main eras of change: the formation of the Solar System and planets; the reworking of crust and mantle and the onset of plate tectonics; and the appearance of life.
After the first era, there were 250 minerals; after the second, 1500.
The remainder were made possible by the action of living organisms, particularly the addition of oxygen to the atmosphere.
This paper was followed over the next few years by several studies concentrating on one chemical element at a time and mapping out the first appearances of minerals involving each element.
A “system of mineralogy” is a classification scheme for all known kinds of minerals.
In 2019, NAME proposed an "evolutionary system of mineralogy," a classification scheme grounded in the concept of mineral evolution and based on the detailed properties of mineral specimens.
The evolutionary system is intended to complement the official mineral classification system of the International Mineralogical Association (IMA) system, which is supervised by the IMA's Commission on New Minerals, Nomenclature and Classification.
Under the IMA system, each mineral species is defined by its unique combination of major-element chemical composition and crystal structure.
The evolutionary system instead groups minerals into "natural kinds" by looking at how they formed, along with their real-world chemistry, structure and physical traits.
This can result in a single IMA species being divided into multiple kinds.
For example, whereas the IMA defines all diamond as one species, the evolutionary system recognizes several kinds of diamond distinguished by origin, including stellar diamond (formed in the atmospheres of stars), mantle diamond (formed in the Earth's deep interior), and impact diamond (formed when an asteroid strikes a carbon-rich deposit).
Conversely, the evolutionary system combines some compositionally similar IMA species into a single kind, such as the magnesium- and iron-rich members of the tourmaline group.
The evolutionary system also includes a variety of non-crystalline materials not recognized as minerals by the IMA, such as obsidian, amber and coal.
The evolutionary system is being published in 12 parts.
