Peter Broomell Rhines (born July 23, 1942) is an American oceanographer and geophysicist who is Professor Emeritus at the University of Washington. He is renowned for his contributions to geophysical fluid dynamics, ocean circulation, and climate science. Rhines is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and a fellow of multiple scientific societies.

 Early life and education 
Peter Broomell Rhines was born on July 23, 1942. He earned his Bachelor of Science (B.S.) and Masters of Science (M.S.) degrees in Aeronautics and Astronautics from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in 1964 and completed his Ph.D. as a Marshall Scholar in Applied Mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge in 1967.

 Academic career 
Rhines joined the faculty of MIT in the department of Meteorology in 1968 as an assistant professor. Beginning in 1972, he was a scientist at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution.

Rhines moved to the University of Washington in 1984 as a professor in Oceanography and Atmospheric Sciences. He became Professor Emeritus in July 2017. His research has focused on understanding large-scale ocean circulation patterns, the dynamics of Rossby waves, and their influence on Earth's climate system. In 2015 the American Geophysical Union sponsored the 20th Conference on Atmospheric and Oceanic Fluid Dynamics. One session was devoted to "The Accomplishments of Peter Rhines".

Rhines has held prestigious positions and fellowships throughout his career:
 Fulbright Scholar at Universidad de Concepción, Chile
 Guggenheim Fellow
 Distinguished Visiting Research Fellow at Christ's College, Cambridge University

 Research contributions 
Rhines is best known for his work on Rossby waves — large-scale waves in Earth's oceans and atmosphere that play a critical role in global circulation patterns. His research has provided new insights into how these waves interact with ocean currents and contribute to climate regulation.

He has also studied eddy dynamics, baroclinic instability, and the impact of topographic features on ocean flow. His findings have been widely applied to improve models of Earth's climate system.

 Research contributions 
Rhines's primary research contributions in geophysical fluid dynamics concern the theory of geostrophic turbulence, large-scale ocean circulation, and the formation of zonal jets on earth and other planets.

 Geostrophic turbulence and the Rhines scale 
In his 1975 paper, "Waves and turbulence on a beta-plane", Rhines addressed the interaction between two-dimensional turbulent cascades and planetary rotation. In two-dimensional and quasi-geostrophic turbulence, an inverse energy cascade transfers kinetic energy up-scale toward larger eddies. Rhines demonstrated that on a beta plane (where Coriolis force varies with latitude via gradient $\beta$), the growth of eddies is bounded when the eddy turnover frequency decreases to match the linear Rossby wave frequency.

Because the dispersion relation for Rossby waves is anisotropic, the arrest of the cascade halts meridional growth while allowing up-scale transfer into zonally elongated structures. This mechanism provides a theoretical explanation for:
 The spontaneous self-organization of turbulent eddies into alternating zonal jets (banded jet streams), famously seen in Jupiter's cloud bands and the atmospheres of other gas giants.
 The meridional spacing and scale selection of eddy-driven currents and striations in Earth's oceans and atmospheric storm tracks.

 Potential vorticity homogenization and ocean gyres 
In a series of papers in the early 1980s with William R. Young, Rhines demonstrated the principle of potential vorticity (PV) homogenization. They showed that within closed streamlines of planetary-scale circulation, weak eddy mixing forces the interior potential vorticity to become uniform. This result superseded classical linear wind-driven models (such as the Sverdrup balance) in regions containing unventilated, closed gyres, and became a cornerstone of modern theories of the oceanic thermocline and ventilated circulation.

 Subpolar climate dynamics and observations 
In addition to his theoretical work in geophysical fluid dynamics, Rhines contributed extensively to physical oceanographic observations, particularly studying the subpolar North Atlantic, convective sinking in the Labrador Sea, deep western boundary currents, and the overturning circulation's role in global climate variability.
